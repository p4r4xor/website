# Edge-Crossing Formulas for Hexagonal Tilings: A Complete Derivation

## Overview

This document derives the formulas for counting how many edges a straight line
from the origin to a lattice point (a, b) crosses in a hexagonal tiling built
from reflected copies of a quadrilateral ABCD with angles 120, 90, 60, 90
degrees. The edges split into two types:

- **Triangle-grid edges** (the CB/CD-type edges, which lie along the three
  families of lines forming the underlying triangular lattice):

$$
\frac{|2a - b|}{3} + \frac{|2b - a|}{3} + \frac{a + b}{3}
$$

- **Hexagon edges** (the AB/AD-type edges, which are interior to the triangles
  and form hexagonal cell boundaries):

$$
\left\lfloor \frac{a}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{b}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{|a - b|}{3} \right\rfloor
$$

The derivation proceeds in five stages: (1) the classical one-dimensional
lattice crossing lemma, (2) extension to three families of lines in a
triangular lattice, (3) the relationship between the triangular lattice and
our hexagonal tiling, (4) the triangle-grid crossing formula, and (5) the
hexagon-edge crossing formula.

---

## 1. The Classical Result: Crossings of a Single Family of Parallel Lines

### 1.1 Setup

Consider the integer lattice in the plane. The vertical lines x = 0, x = 1,
x = 2, ... form a family of parallel lines with unit spacing.

**Lemma 1.1.** A straight line segment from the origin (0, 0) to the point
(p, q) with p a positive integer and gcd(p, q) = 1 crosses exactly p - 1
vertical lattice lines (the lines x = 1, x = 2, ..., x = p - 1). If
gcd(p, q) = d > 1, the segment passes through d - 1 lattice points in its
interior, and we count p - 1 crossings of lines x = k for k = 1, ..., p - 1
regardless.

More generally, for a family of parallel lines with spacing s perpendicular
to a direction, a segment whose projection onto that direction has length L
crosses floor(L/s) lines in general position (not counting endpoints).

**Proof.** The line from (0, 0) to (p, q) is parametrized as (tp, tq) for
t in [0, 1]. It crosses the line x = k when tp = k, i.e., t = k/p. For
k = 1, 2, ..., p - 1, these are p - 1 distinct crossing events in the
interior of the segment.

This elementary fact is the building block for all that follows.

### 1.2 Two families: the square lattice

For the standard square lattice with horizontal lines y = k and vertical
lines x = k, the segment from (0, 0) to (p, q) crosses:

- |p| - 1 vertical lines (not counting endpoints)
- |q| - 1 horizontal lines

This generalizes in the obvious way. The key insight for triangular lattices
is that we simply have *three* families instead of two.

## 2. Three Families of Parallel Lines: The Triangular Lattice

### 2.1 The equilateral triangular tessellation

An equilateral triangular tessellation T of the plane is generated by three
families of equally-spaced parallel lines at mutual 60-degree angles. Following
Baxter and Umble (Section 2, "Orbits and Tessellations"), the tessellation is
oriented so that one family of edges is horizontal.

We use **rhombic coordinates** as defined in Baxter and Umble, Section 3.
Let O be a chosen origin on a horizontal edge. The coordinate axes are:

- The **x-axis**: the horizontal line through O
- The **y-axis**: the line through O with inclination 60 degrees

The unit of length is the side length of the triangles (equivalently, the
spacing between adjacent parallel lines of the same family). A point with
rhombic coordinates (x, y) has Cartesian position:

$$
X_{\text{cart}} = x + \frac{y}{2}, \qquad Y_{\text{cart}} = \frac{y \sqrt{3}}{2}
$$

In rhombic coordinates, the three families of lines comprising the triangular
lattice are:

| Family | Direction | Equation | Spacing in rhombic coords |
|--------|-----------|----------|--------------------------|
| Horizontal | along (1, 0) | y = k, k in Z | 1 in y |
| Left-leaning (60 deg) | along (0, 1) | x = k, k in Z | 1 in x |
| Right-leaning (120 deg) | along (1, -1) | x + y = k equivalently y - x = k | 1 in x+y |

Wait -- let us be more precise. The three families, in rhombic coordinates,
have the following characterization. A point (x, y) lies on:

- A **horizontal** edge of T when y is an integer.
- A **left-leaning** edge (at 60 deg from horizontal) when x is an integer.
- A **right-leaning** edge (at 120 deg from horizontal) when x - y is an integer
  (equivalently, when the line is of the form x - y = const).

### 2.2 Counting crossings in rhombic coordinates

**Theorem 2.1.** A straight line segment from the origin O = (0, 0) to a
point S = (x, y) in rhombic coordinates crosses:

- |y| lines of the horizontal family (lines of constant y-coordinate)
- |x| lines of the left-leaning family (lines of constant x-coordinate)
- |x - y| lines of the right-leaning family (lines of constant x - y)

when gcd is 1 and we do not count endpoints.

**Proof.** The parametric line is (tx, ty) for t in [0, 1].

*Horizontal crossings:* The line crosses y = k when ty = k, i.e., t = k/y.
For k = 1, ..., |y| - 1 (if y > 0) plus possibly k = y at the endpoint,
there are |y| - 1 interior crossings. But following the convention of counting
the crossing at the far endpoint (which is a lattice point of the tessellation),
the standard count for the number of edges of T crossed is |y|. (More
precisely: the segment from O to S crosses |y| horizontal edges if we count
each horizontal line y = k for k = 1, ..., y that the segment crosses, but
the crossing at y itself may or may not be a "true" edge crossing depending on
whether S is a vertex. For lines in general position, the count is |y|.)

Similarly for the other two families: the segment crosses |x| left-leaning
lines and |x - y| right-leaning lines.

This is the direct analog of the classical result, applied independently to
each of the three families. The argument works because in rhombic coordinates,
each family of lines is characterized by one linear functional taking integer
values, and the segment's projection onto that functional goes from 0 to the
value at S.

### 2.3 Connection to billiards: the unfolding method

Baxter and Umble (Section 2) show that a billiard trajectory on an equilateral
triangle unfolds to a straight line segment in the triangular tessellation T.
The number of times the trajectory bounces off each side of the triangle equals
the number of times the unfolded line crosses lines of the corresponding
family. This is the standard "unfolding" technique for rational billiards.

Specifically, from Baxter and Umble, Corollary 3 (Section 3):

> If S_alpha = (x, y), then alpha has period 2(x + y).

The period 2n equals twice the number of rhombic tiles cut by the fundamental
unfolding OS. Each rhombic tile is cut when the line crosses an edge, and
the total number of edges crossed is x + y (horizontal plus one family of
non-horizontal). But actually the period counts *all* edges of the
tessellation that are crossed, which is x + y for the particular way Baxter
and Umble set up their coordinates relative to the rhombic tiling.

This is the key insight from the Baxter-Umble paper: **the billiard period
on the equilateral triangle is directly read off from rhombic coordinates**.

## 3. From Triangular Lattice to Hexagonal Tiling

### 3.1 The tiling structure

The hexagonal tiling in Euler Problem 786 arises from repeatedly reflecting
the quadrilateral ABCD (angles 120, 90, 60, 90 degrees) across its edges.
As hos-lyric states in the Euler 786 thread:

> "By repeatedly reflecting the quadrilateral, we have the planar tiling with
> regular hexagons. The vertices of the hexagons correspond to A, the centers
> of the hexagons to C, the midpoints of the edges of the hexagons to B or D."

And as semiexp clarifies:

> "This tiling basically looks like that with the equilateral triangles, but
> each triangle is divided into 3 parts."

The relationship is:

1. Start with the **regular triangular tessellation** T (equilateral triangles
   tiling the plane).

2. Each equilateral triangle in T is subdivided into **3 copies** of the
   quadrilateral ABCD. The vertex A sits at a vertex of the triangle, C sits
   at the centroid, and B, D sit at edge midpoints.

3. Equivalently, the tiling has **two kinds of edges**:
   - **Triangle-grid edges**: These are the CB and CD edges of the
     quadrilaterals. They lie along the three families of parallel lines of T.
     Each edge of the original triangular lattice is subdivided into two
     half-edges (from a midpoint B or D to the centroid C of an adjacent
     triangle).
   - **Hexagon edges**: These are the AB and AD edges of the quadrilaterals.
     They connect triangle vertices (A-type points) to edge midpoints (B/D
     type points). Six such edges surround each A-vertex, forming a regular
     hexagon.

4. The **hexagonal tessellation** H (by regular hexagons) is the dual
   structure: hexagons are centered on the A-vertices of the triangular
   lattice (which form a sublattice), and the hexagon edges are exactly the
   AB/AD edges described above.

### 3.2 Coordinate system

Following the Euler 786 thread (hos-lyric and semiexp), we place vertex A at
the **origin** and define basis vectors:

$$
\vec{s} = \overrightarrow{AB}, \qquad \vec{t} = \overrightarrow{AD}
$$

These two vectors have equal length and an angle of 120 degrees between them
(since the angle at A in the quadrilateral is 120 degrees, and ABCD tiles
around A with six copies making a full 360-degree turn -- three pairs of
reflected quadrilaterals giving 3 x 120 = 360 degrees).

A target point P reachable from A through the tiling is expressed as:

$$
P = 2a \cdot \vec{s} + 2b \cdot \vec{t}
$$

where a, b are positive integers. The factor of 2 arises because each
reflection across an edge of the quadrilateral moves the point by one
edge-length, and it takes *two* reflections (there and back) to return to the
same orientation. The coordinates (a, b) are essentially **half** of the
rhombic coordinates of the point in the triangular lattice when measured in
units of the edge midpoint spacing.

### 3.3 Relationship between (a, b) and the triangular lattice

The triangular lattice T has three families of parallel lines. In the (a, b)
coordinate system, the spacing of each family corresponds to a period of 3
in the coordinates, because **each triangle is subdivided into 3
quadrilaterals**. Specifically:

- The segment from origin to (2a, 2b) (in the s, t basis) traverses a
  distance that spans multiple triangles. Since each triangle has been
  subdivided into 3 quadrilateral cells, the natural lattice spacing measured
  in (a, b) units has period 3.

- The lattice L of points corresponding to images of the origin O under the
  hexagonal tiling translations is generated by vectors (1, 1) and (0, 3) in
  (a, b) coordinates (Baxter-Umble, Section 3). A point (x, y) belongs to L
  if and only if x = y (mod 3).

- The constraint a + b = 0 (mod 3) ensures the target point P is again an
  A-type vertex (i.e., it has 3 outgoing edges and corresponds to A in the
  tiling), as stated by both hos-lyric and semiexp.

## 4. Triangle-Grid Edge Crossings

### 4.1 Geometry of the CB/CD edges

The quadrilateral ABCD has angles A = 120, B = 90, C = 60, D = 90 degrees.
Within each equilateral triangle of the tessellation T (with vertices V1, V2,
V3), the quadrilateral sits as:

- A = V1 (a triangle vertex)
- B = midpoint of edge V1V2
- C = centroid of the triangle
- D = midpoint of edge V1V3

The edges AB and AD lie **along** triangle edges (they are half-edges from
vertex to midpoint). The edges BC and CD connect edge midpoints to the
centroid. As hos-lyric states in the Euler 786 thread:

> "Edges (corresponding to CB or CD) orthogonal to AD: (2a-b)/3"
> "Edges (corresponding to CB or CD) orthogonal to AB: |2b-a|/3"
> "Edges (corresponding to CB or CD) orthogonal to AD + AB: (a+b)/3"

The CB/CD edges are organized into three families, each **perpendicular** to
one of the three principal directions s, t, and s+t. This is geometrically
natural: in an equilateral triangle, the segment from the centroid to the
midpoint of a side is perpendicular to that side. Since the three sides are
parallel to s, t, and s+t respectively, the centroid-to-midpoint segments
are perpendicular to these directions.

The AB/AD edges (half-edges lying along triangle sides) are the **hexagon
edges**, treated separately in Section 5.

### 4.2 The three linear functionals via projections

Working in Cartesian coordinates with s = (1, 0) and t = (-1/2, sqrt(3)/2),
the point P = 2a*s + 2b*t has coordinates:

$$
P = (2a - b, \; b\sqrt{3})
$$

The three families of CB/CD edges are perpendicular to s, t, and s+t. To
count crossings, we compute the **signed projection** of P onto each of these
directions:

**Projection onto s = (1, 0):**
$$
P \cdot \hat{s} = 2a - b
$$

**Projection onto t = (-1/2, sqrt(3)/2):**
$$
P \cdot \hat{t} = (2a - b)(-1/2) + b\sqrt{3} \cdot \sqrt{3}/2 = -a + b/2 + 3b/2 = -a + 2b = -(2a - b) + 3b - b
$$
Simplifying: the projection is 2b - a (up to sign).

**Projection onto (s + t) = (1/2, sqrt(3)/2):**
$$
P \cdot \widehat{(s+t)} = (2a - b)(1/2) + b\sqrt{3} \cdot \sqrt{3}/2 = a - b/2 + 3b/2 = a + b
$$

### 4.3 The spacing of 3 and the division factor

The CB/CD edges perpendicular to each direction are arranged in parallel rows.
The **spacing** between consecutive rows, measured in the projection
coordinates above, is **3**.

This factor of 3 arises directly from the subdivision structure:

1. Each equilateral triangle has side length 2 in our units (since |AB| =
   |s| = 1, and the full triangle edge spans from A to the next A-vertex,
   a distance of 2|s|).

2. Each triangle is subdivided into **3 quadrilateral cells**. The three
   centroids of the three triangles meeting at an edge are separated by
   specific distances.

3. In the (a, b) coordinate system, the sublattice of A-type vertices has
   **index 3** in the full integer lattice (Baxter-Umble, Section 3: the
   sublattice L is generated by (1,1) and (0,3), and (x, y) in L iff
   x = y mod 3). This means the fundamental domain of the A-lattice has
   3 times the area of a single (a,b) unit cell.

4. Consequently, the CB/CD edges of a given family repeat with period 3
   in the projection coordinates.

The number of rows of CB/CD edges crossed by the segment from O to P is
therefore the projection value divided by 3:

| Family | Perpendicular to | Projection | Crossing count |
|--------|-----------------|------------|----------------|
| 1 | s | 2a - b | \|2a - b\|/3 |
| 2 | t | 2b - a | \|2b - a\|/3 |
| 3 | s + t | a + b | (a + b)/3 |

### 4.4 Integrality check

For these counts to be integers, we need 2a - b, 2b - a, and a + b all
divisible by 3. Given the constraint a + b = 0 (mod 3):

- 2a - b = 3a - (a + b) = 0 (mod 3)
- 2b - a = 3b - (a + b) = 0 (mod 3)

So all three terms are integers whenever the target is a valid A-type vertex.

### 4.5 Concrete verification

Take a = 3, b = 0 (valid since a + b = 3 = 0 mod 3, and P = 6s lies along
the s-axis):

- |2(3) - 0|/3 = 2 crossings perpendicular to s
- |2(0) - 3|/3 = 1 crossing perpendicular to t
- (3 + 0)/3 = 1 crossing perpendicular to s + t
- Total triangle-grid: 4

Take a = 2, b = 1 (a + b = 3 = 0 mod 3):

- |4 - 1|/3 = 1
- |2 - 2|/3 = 0
- (2 + 1)/3 = 1
- Total triangle-grid: 2

This matches: the direction (2, 1) is close to the s-direction, crosses 1
family-1 edge and 1 family-3 edge, but runs nearly parallel to family-2 edges.

### 4.6 Summary: the division by 3

**The factor of 1/3 in the triangle-grid crossing formulas arises because the
(a, b) coordinates are 3 times finer than the triangular lattice spacing.**

Each equilateral triangle in T spans 3 units in the (a, b) coordinates
along each principal direction. This is because each triangle is subdivided
into 3 quadrilateral cells, and moving from one A-vertex to an adjacent
A-vertex requires traversing 3 quadrilateral cells. In the language of
Baxter-Umble's lattice theory (Section 3): the sublattice L of points
congruent to the origin is generated by vectors (1,1) and (0,3), and the
index of L in the full integer lattice Z^2 is 3.

The **total triangle-grid crossing count** is:

$$
\boxed{\frac{|2a - b|}{3} + \frac{|2b - a|}{3} + \frac{a + b}{3}}
$$

## 5. Hexagon Edge Crossings

### 5.1 The hexagon edges

The hexagon edges are the AB and AD type edges of the quadrilateral tiling.
They connect A-type vertices (triangle vertices) to B/D-type vertices
(triangle edge midpoints). Six such edges emanate from each A-vertex,
forming a regular hexagon around it.

The hexagon edges are **not** perpendicular to any of the three main
directions; instead, they lie **along** the three edge directions of the
triangular lattice (since AB and AD lie along triangle edges -- they are
half-edges from a vertex to the midpoint of the adjacent edge).

### 5.2 Why the floor function appears

Unlike the triangle-grid edges (which form continuous lines across the entire
plane), the hexagon edges are **discrete segments** that occur in a
periodic pattern. A hexagon edge parallel to s occurs at specific positions
in the lattice, and whether the line from O to P crosses such an edge depends
on a **modular arithmetic** condition.

Specifically, consider hexagon edges parallel to the s-direction (= AB-type
edges). These edges connect A-vertices at integer (a, b) coordinates to
B-vertices at half-integer positions. Along any line of the triangular lattice
in the s-direction, hexagon edges occur with period 3: out of every 3
consecutive "cells" along the s-direction, exactly 1 contains a hexagon edge.

The crossing count for hexagon edges in the s-family is:

$$
\left\lfloor \frac{a}{3} \right\rfloor
$$

The floor function arises because the hexagon edges occur at positions
k*3 + offset (for integer k) along the a-coordinate, and the number of such
positions in the range [1, a-1] is exactly floor((a - offset)/3) which equals
floor(a/3) for appropriate offset when a + b ≡ 0 (mod 3).

### 5.3 Derivation via the Euclidean algorithm

The most rigorous approach to counting hexagon edge crossings uses the
**Euclidean algorithm** method, as described by xiaoyaowudi in the Euler 786
thread. The idea is:

For each family of hexagon edges (parallel to s, parallel to t, parallel to
s+t), the edges lie along certain lines, and within each line, the edges
occupy specific intervals. The segment from O to P crosses a hexagon edge on
a given line if and only if the intersection point falls within one of these
edge intervals.

For the family parallel to the s-direction, the edges occur periodically
with period 3 in the perpendicular coordinate. Specifically, along a
horizontal line at height corresponding to some value of b, hexagon edges
parallel to s exist at positions where a ≡ r (mod 3) for a specific residue r
that depends on the line. The parametric line from O to (a, b) crosses these
with a frequency determined by the Euclidean-algorithm-type analysis of the
rational slope b/a modulo 3.

### 5.4 The formula and its derivation

**Claim (from the Euler 786 thread, verified empirically by semiexp and given
explicitly by Doraki):**

The total number of hexagon edges crossed equals:

$$
\left\lfloor \frac{a}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{b}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{|a - b|}{3} \right\rfloor
$$

**Derivation:**

The hexagon edges come in three families, parallel to the three triangle-edge
directions. For each family, we count crossings independently.

**Family parallel to s (AB-type edges along the s-direction):**

An AB-edge along the s-direction connects a vertex at (a_0, b_0) to the
midpoint at (a_0 + 1, b_0). These edges exist at positions where a_0 is
an A-type vertex. The A-type vertices are at positions where a_0 + b_0 ≡ 0
(mod 3).

The segment from (0,0) to (a, b) crosses an edge of this family when it
crosses a short segment at height b_0 (in t-coordinate) at the appropriate
a-coordinate. The number of such crossings is determined by how many times
the segment enters a new "hexagonal cell" in the direction perpendicular to s.

The hexagonal cells perpendicular to s have period 3 in the b-coordinate.
The number of cell boundaries crossed is:

$$
\left\lfloor \frac{b}{3} \right\rfloor
$$

This is because the hexagon edges parallel to s are separated by 3 units
in the b-coordinate. Each crossing of such an edge occurs when b passes
through a multiple of 3, which happens floor(b/3) times in the interval
[0, b].

**Family parallel to t (AD-type edges along the t-direction):**

By the same argument with a and b swapped:

$$
\left\lfloor \frac{a}{3} \right\rfloor
$$

**Family parallel to s + t:**

These edges are parallel to the diagonal direction. The relevant coordinate
perpendicular to s + t is a - b (or |a - b|). The crossing count is:

$$
\left\lfloor \frac{|a - b|}{3} \right\rfloor
$$

**Total hexagon edge crossings:**

$$
\boxed{\left\lfloor \frac{a}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{b}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{|a - b|}{3} \right\rfloor}
$$

### 5.5 Why floor and not exact division?

The floor function appears (instead of exact division by 3) because the
hexagon edge positions depend on **both** coordinates modulo 3, not just one.
Unlike the triangle-grid edges, which lie on continuous lines spanning the
entire plane (so the crossing count is purely determined by the net
displacement), hexagon edges are short segments that may or may not be
present at a given crossing line.

When the segment from O to P crosses a line that *could* contain a hexagon
edge, it does so only if the specific position modulo 3 is correct. The
cumulative count over the entire path is:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{b} \mathbf{1}[\text{hexagon edge of s-family exists at this crossing}]
$$

The indicator function averages to 1/3, but the actual count is not b/3
exactly -- it is floor(b/3) or floor(b/3) + 1 depending on b mod 3. Since
a + b ≡ 0 (mod 3), the exact values work out to floor functions.

### 5.6 Verification of the combined formula

For the Euler 786 problem, Doraki confirms the total bounce count is:

$$
\frac{|2a-b|}{3} + \frac{|2b-a|}{3} + \frac{a+b}{3} + \left\lfloor \frac{a}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{b}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{|a-b|}{3} \right\rfloor
$$

This can be verified against hos-lyric's more case-specific formula. For
a > b (WLOG by symmetry), hos-lyric gives the hexagon edge count as:

$$
\frac{2a - 4}{3} \quad \text{if } b \equiv 1 \pmod{3}, \qquad \frac{2a - 5}{3} \quad \text{if } b \equiv 2 \pmod{3}
$$

Let us check that this agrees with floor(a/3) + floor(b/3) + floor((a-b)/3).

Since a + b ≡ 0 (mod 3) and a > b > 0, we have two cases:

**Case b ≡ 1 (mod 3):** Then a ≡ 2 (mod 3) (since a + b ≡ 0 mod 3).
- floor(a/3) = (a - 2)/3
- floor(b/3) = (b - 1)/3
- a - b ≡ 2 - 1 = 1 (mod 3), so floor((a-b)/3) = (a - b - 1)/3
- Sum = (a - 2)/3 + (b - 1)/3 + (a - b - 1)/3 = (2a - 4)/3

This matches hos-lyric's formula.

**Case b ≡ 2 (mod 3):** Then a ≡ 1 (mod 3).
- floor(a/3) = (a - 1)/3
- floor(b/3) = (b - 2)/3
- a - b ≡ 1 - 2 = -1 ≡ 2 (mod 3), so floor((a-b)/3) = (a - b - 2)/3
- Sum = (a - 1)/3 + (b - 2)/3 + (a - b - 2)/3 = (2a - 5)/3

This also matches. The agreement confirms the formula.

## 6. Summary and Complete Formula

### 6.1 Setup

Given the hexagonal tiling formed by reflecting quadrilateral ABCD (angles
120, 90, 60, 90 degrees), with basis vectors s = AB and t = AD, a valid
target point is P = 2a*s + 2b*t where:

- a, b are positive integers
- a + b ≡ 0 (mod 3) (ensures P is an A-type vertex)
- gcd(a, b) = 1 (ensures no A-type vertex lies in the interior of the segment)

### 6.2 Triangle-grid edges (CB/CD type)

These arise from three families of parallel line segments, each perpendicular
to one of the three principal directions. The counts are computed from the
**three linear functionals** of the triangular lattice, divided by 3 due to
the 3:1 subdivision ratio:

| Family | Perpendicular to | Functional | Count |
|--------|-----------------|------------|-------|
| 1 | s (= AD direction) | 2a - b | \|2a - b\|/3 |
| 2 | t (= AB direction) | 2b - a | \|2b - a\|/3 |
| 3 | s + t | a + b | (a + b)/3 |

**Total triangle-grid crossings:**

$$
\frac{|2a - b|}{3} + \frac{|2b - a|}{3} + \frac{a + b}{3}
$$

### 6.3 Hexagon edges (AB/AD type)

These arise from three families of short segments parallel to the three
principal directions. The counts involve floor functions because the edges
are discrete (period-3 pattern):

| Family | Parallel to | Coordinate | Count |
|--------|------------|------------|-------|
| 1 | s | b | floor(b/3) |
| 2 | t | a | floor(a/3) |
| 3 | s + t | \|a - b\| | floor(\|a - b\|/3) |

Note the transposition: edges *parallel* to s are counted by the *perpendicular*
coordinate b (and vice versa), because you cross edges parallel to s when
moving in the t-direction, and the edges are spaced every 3 units in that
perpendicular coordinate.

**Total hexagon-edge crossings:**

$$
\left\lfloor \frac{a}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{b}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{|a - b|}{3} \right\rfloor
$$

### 6.4 Grand total

$$
\text{Total bounces} = \underbrace{\frac{|2a - b|}{3} + \frac{|2b - a|}{3} + \frac{a + b}{3}}_{\text{triangle-grid edges}} + \underbrace{\left\lfloor \frac{a}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{b}{3} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{|a - b|}{3} \right\rfloor}_{\text{hexagon edges}}
$$

## 7. Connection to Project Euler #202

Project Euler Problem 202 is the simpler version: counting laser beam
reflections in an equilateral triangle. In that problem, the tiling is just
the equilateral triangular tessellation T (no subdivision into quadrilaterals).
The unfolded beam is a straight line in T, and the bounce count equals the
number of triangle edges crossed.

In Baxter-Umble's rhombic coordinates, a beam to lattice point (x, y) has
period 2(x + y) and crosses:

- x left-leaning edges
- y horizontal edges
- |x - y| right-leaning edges

Total: x + y + |x - y| = 2 max(x, y) edges, which equals the period minus 2
(accounting for the two endpoint bounces).

The Euler 202 counting formula (as derived by many solvers) involves:
- Finding all lattice points (x, y) with x + y = n (the period divided by 2)
- Requiring x ≡ y (mod 3) (to exit through the correct vertex)
- Requiring gcd(x, y) = 1 (to avoid hitting a vertex en route)

This is precisely the framework from Baxter-Umble, Theorem 3, which
establishes the bijection between orbit classes of period 2n and lattice
points (x, y) with x + y = n, x ≡ y (mod 3), 0 <= x <= y.

The Euler 786 problem generalizes this to the quadrilateral ABCD, where the
bounce count has the more complex formula involving both triangle-grid and
hexagon edge crossings derived above.
